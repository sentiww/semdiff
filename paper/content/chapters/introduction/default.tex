\section{Dangers of Classifier Errors}

\ac{ai} systems are now deployed at scale across real-world, decision-critical domains, such as autonomous driving or healthcare.
As model outputs increasingly drive critical decisions, accuracy and robustness to misclassification become essential.

At the same time, achieving robust behavior remains difficult.
Most classifiers are trained on closed-world datasets and are primarily optimized for \ac{id} data.
Which may causes models to produce highly confident predictions that are incorrect in classification of \ac{ood} data.

Conventional model evaluation typically aggregates all such misclassifications into a single error count.
This does not account for whether an error is between semantically similar classes or semantically dissimilar classes,
despite the fact that these outcomes can imply different practical risk.
Recognizing this motivates research into the classification errors profiles of different models.

Classification errors are not made equal, either in their cause or thier consequences.
It is useful to distinguish errors that occur between semantically related classes from errors that occur between semantically distant classes.
For example, in case of an autonomous vehicle, confusing two visually similar vehicle categories may be less consequential than confusing a pedestrian with a background object.
Even though both cases are counted identically in standard accuracy metrics, they can differ substantially in practical severity.

This suggests that error analysis should not only take into account whether a prediction is correct, but also for how wrong it is in semantic terms.
A semantic analysis of classification can reveal systematic model biases that remain hidden in standard error rates.
Such a view also supports more informed model improvement strategies, such as data curation and training with greater penalizes for high-impact confusions.

\section{Research Goal and Scope}

The goal of this work is to develop a set of tools for analysis of classification errors according to semantic similarity between predicted and target classes.
Rather than treating all misclassifications as equal, the idea is to investigate whether a semantic taxonomy provides additional explanatory value to errors.
The central research question is therefore: to what extent do semantics of errors improve the interpretation of model behavior?

The scope of this study is limited to image classification models evaluated on existing, labeled benchmark datasets.
Analysis will be performed on model prediction outcomes and is purely post hoc, models are not modified in any way.
The objective is not to replace standard model performance metrics, but to extend them with semantic information.

\section{Main Contribution}

This thesis makes three main contributions.
First, it introduces a principled distinction between semantically similar and semantically dissimilar misclassifications, and formalizes this distinction for empirical analysis.
Second, it proposes an evaluation workflow that integrates conventional performance measures with semantics-aware error characterization.
Third, it provides an empirical study demonstrating how this perspective yields additional insight into model errors.

Together, these contributions allow for a more detailed interpretation of classifier reliability and motivate the inclusion of semantic error structure in the model assessment process.
